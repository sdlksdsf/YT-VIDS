Across the mouth of the Golden Horn, the Byzantines stretched a great chain — iron links floating on logs — from the city to the Genoese colony of Galata on the north side. No ship could enter. As long as the chain held, the city had a safe harbor, and as long as the harbor was safe, the city could be resupplied. It was the second trump card. So now, count the cards. The walls. The chain. The harbor. Against the knights, the cannon, and the largest army in Europe. Two men. One city. And a clock that was already ticking. On April 6th, 1453, the guns opened fire. The siege of Constantinople had begun. Here's the rhythm of the next few weeks: the Ottomans fire. A tower crumbles. A gap opens. The defenders wait for the smoke to clear — and then they run out with rubble, earth, and timber, and they plug the hole. At night. In the rain. Under arrow fire. Then the guns fire again. It was a race between the cannon and the shovel. And for a long time, the shovel was winning. At sea, the other problem. The Ottoman fleet — around a hundred ships — had one job: keep the chain closed. And for weeks, the chain did its job. The fleet threw itself at it and bounced off. Then, on April 20th, four Christian ships came up the Sea of Marmara. A small convoy — Genoese galleys and a Byzantine grain ship. The whole Ottoman navy moved to stop them. And the four ships fought their way through and into the harbor. The defenders cheered.
