Mehmed rode in and, by most accounts, stopped the worst of it. In the Hagia Sophia, he saw a ghazi smashing the marble floor and asked what he was doing. 'It is for the Faith!' the man said. Mehmed cut him down: 'Be satisfied with the booty and the captives; the buildings of the city belong to me.' He had the great church converted into a mosque. He appointed a new patriarch for his Christian subjects. He offered amnesty to those who came out of hiding. And two days later, standing before a severed head said to be Constantine's, he told his soldiers: 'We have the riches of the Greeks, we have won their empire, and their religion is completely extinguished.' So what did the fall of Constantinople actually change? More than you'd think. It ended the Roman Empire. Not the 'Byzantine' Empire — that's a word historians invented later. The people in that city called themselves Roman, and they had for a thousand years. When Constantine died, the last living link to Augustus was cut. It ended the Middle Ages. Historians still argue about the date, but 1453 is one of the big three — along with 1492 and 1517. Walls had defined war for two thousand years. After Constantinople, walls were just obstacles. It fed the Renaissance. Greek scholars fled to Italy with books and knowledge that had been forgotten in the West. The engine of the Renaissance got a new fuel tank. It pushed Europe outward.
