'He'll be easy.' Mehmed smiled, signed friendly treaties, and then did exactly what everyone should have expected: he started planning to take the greatest city in Christendom. He had been thinking about it since he was a prince. Constantinople was the key — to the Black Sea, to the Balkans, to the trade routes, to history. Whoever held it could call himself Roman. And Mehmed intended to. Mehmed's first move was pure geography. In early 1452, he ordered a fortress built on the European shore of the Bosphorus, four miles north of Constantinople — directly across from an older Ottoman fort on the Asian side. Together, they would strangle the strait. The Turks called it Boğazkesen: 'the throat-cutter.' The Byzantines had a less funny name for it: a noose. It went up in about four months. Four months. And to show it was open for business, Mehmed's guns sank a Venetian ship that refused to stop. The message was simple: the old rules were over. Now, a fortress is only half a siege. To break the walls, Mehmed needed a monster. And a monster needed a maker. Enter Orban — a Hungarian engineer with a dream and a price tag. He walked into Constantinople and told the emperor: I can build you a cannon that will smash any wall ever built. Constantine asked how much. Orban named a number. The emperor did the math — the treasury was empty — and said no. So Orban walked out of the city, crossed the lines, and knocked on the sultan's door.
