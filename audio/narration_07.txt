Serbian sappers, brought in from the silver mines of Novo Brdo, started tunneling under the walls. If they could collapse a tower from below, the cannon wouldn't matter. But the defenders had their own engineer — a German named Johannes Grant, who came with the Genoese. He dug counter-mines. He listened. He intercepted the first tunnel on the night of May 16th. Then another on the 21st. Another on the 23rd. Another on the 25th. On May 23rd, they captured two Ottoman officers, and under torture the officers gave up the locations of every tunnel. The Byzantines burned them out with Greek fire. The walls were holding. The tunnels were failing. And Mehmed was running out of time — and patience. And then the sky itself seemed to give up. On May 22nd, there was a partial lunar eclipse. To the defenders, it felt like a prophecy. Four days later, a thick fog blanketed the city — a fog nobody in that part of the world could remember in May. And when it lifted that evening, a strange light was seen playing over the dome of Hagia Sophia. Some said it was the Holy Spirit leaving. Others said it was the campfires of a relief army that was never coming. We now know it was probably volcanic — a massive eruption somewhere on the planet had filled the sky with ash. But in 1453, it felt like the end of the world. Which, for the people in that city, was not a metaphor.
