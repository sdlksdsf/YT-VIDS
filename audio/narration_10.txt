But something happened at that gate, because suddenly the defenders saw an enemy flag on the walls, and the panic became a collapse. Constantine XI Palaiologos was last seen alive doing something very specific. He stripped off his imperial regalia — the purple, the badges of office — so that no one could tell which body was the emperor's. And then he charged into the enemy. One source says he died in the street. Another says he took his own life at the moment the walls broke. His body was never certainly identified. But the last Roman emperor died as he had lived: standing in a gap, holding a wall that could no longer be held. By mid-morning, it was over. What happened next is the part Mehmed later cried about. His soldiers had been promised three days of plunder — it was the custom of the time. They took the city street by street. The houses nearest the walls first. Then the great church. The Hagia Sophia's bronze doors were barred from inside by a huge crowd praying for protection. The doors were broken open. The crowd was sorted — by age, by beauty, by what price they would bring. Around thirty thousand people were enslaved. The Venetian captain ordered his men to break open the harbor gate, and the Venetians — soldiers and refugees — escaped by sea. The Genoese and even the emperor's own ships followed, narrowly getting out before the Ottoman navy closed the Horn at midday.
