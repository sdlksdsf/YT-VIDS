On the morning of May 29th, 1453, the most famous city in the world died. For a thousand years, its walls had never been breached. Its emperors called themselves Roman. Its enemies called it the Queen of Cities. And on this Tuesday morning, a 21-year-old sultan walked through its gates and turned its greatest church into a mosque. But this is not a story about a wall. It's a story about two young men who wanted the same thing — and only one of them could win. This… is the last 53 days of Rome. Let's start at the beginning. Not 1453 — the real beginning. In 330 AD, the Roman emperor Constantine the Great built a new capital on the Bosphorus and named it after himself: Constantinople. For eleven hundred years, it was the richest, most defended, most beautiful city on Earth. Invaders came and broke themselves on its walls like waves. By 1453, that empire was a ghost. Plague, civil war, and four centuries of losing had shrunk it to a single city with fields inside its own walls. The population? Maybe fifty thousand — down from half a million. The empire's borders? The city walls. That's it. The rest was the Ottoman Empire. And those walls. Oh, those walls. The Theodosian Walls: a moat sixty feet wide, an outer wall, an inner wall, towers every fifty meters. They had never fallen. Not to the Huns, not to the Arabs, not to the Bulgarians, not to the Crusaders — well, the Crusaders got in, but only because someone opened a gate.
