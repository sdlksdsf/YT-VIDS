On May 21st, Mehmed made one last offer: surrender the city, and the emperor and everyone who wanted to leave could go, with their possessions. He'd even let Constantine rule the Peloponnese. Constantine's answer has survived for five hundred years: 'As to surrendering the city to you, it is not for me to decide or for anyone else of its citizens; for all of us have reached the mutual decision to die of our own free will, without any regard for our lives.' In the Ottoman camp, the old vizier Halil Pasha said: abandon the siege. The attacker Zagan Pasha said: no — one more push. Mehmed chose the push. He ordered a final, all-out assault. And he gave his men thirty-six hours to prepare. On the night of May 28th, the city did something extraordinary. It held a last church service. In the Hagia Sophia — the great church of Holy Wisdom, nine hundred years old — the emperor, the nobility, the priests, the soldiers, the women and children, all came together. Orthodox and Catholic, Greek and Italian, side by side. They sang the vespers. They took communion. They said goodbye to their city. Nobody slept. The cannons had fired five thousand shots. Fifty-five thousand pounds of gunpowder. The walls were wounded everywhere. And out in the dark, the Ottoman camp was silent — an enormous, waiting silence. Shortly after midnight, the final assault began. Wave one: the Christian troops of the Ottoman army. Thrown at the walls, thrown back.
