He told Mehmed the same thing, plus a bonus: this cannon could blast 'the walls of Babylon itself.' Mehmed didn't haggle. He paid, he funded, he gave the man a foundry at Edirne and all the bronze, wood, and labor he needed. Three months later, the Great Bombard existed. Eight meters long. A bore of thirty inches. It threw a stone ball weighing six hundred kilos — over a mile. It took sixty oxen and four hundred men to drag it. It could fire maybe seven times a day, because the bronze needed hours to cool. And it cracked under its own recoil after about six weeks. It was, in other words, a terrible weapon. And it was exactly what Mehmed needed. Because the goal wasn't to be efficient. The goal was to make the walls bleed — a little more every single day. Getting it there was its own epic. The roads had to be rebuilt to carry the weight. Fifty carpenters and two hundred artisans strengthened bridges. The gun crawled toward the city while, inside, the defenders made their own preparations. In January 1453, a Genoese soldier-for-hire named Giovanni Giustiniani arrived with seven hundred men. He was a specialist in one thing: defending walled cities. Constantine put him in charge of the land walls. It was the best decision he made all year. Giustiniani's second gift was the harbor.
