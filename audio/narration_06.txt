That night, Mehmed made a decision that would go down as one of the great 'why didn't we think of that' moments of military history. He had a road of greased logs built over the hill behind Galata. And on April 22nd, his men hauled about seventy ships — up the slope, over the top, and down into the Golden Horn. Behind the chain. Imagine being a defender on the sea wall that morning. You've been holding the land walls for over two weeks. You're exhausted. And then you look north and see the enemy fleet — sailing into your harbor. From behind you. The chain, the great trump card, was suddenly a decoration. Now the defenders had to guard the sea walls too. And they were already out of men. What followed was the ugly middle of the siege. The defenders tried to burn the Ottoman ships in the Horn — fire ships, a night attack. It failed. Forty Italians escaped the sinking ships and swam to the far shore. Mehmed had them impaled on stakes, in sight of the walls. The defenders responded by dragging their own prisoners — two hundred and sixty of them — up to the walls and executing them, one by one, in sight of the Ottomans. This is the part of the story that doesn't fit the cartoon. It was brutal, and it was personal, and it was about to get worse. Because the Ottomans had one more trick: the miners.
