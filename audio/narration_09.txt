Wave two: the irregulars, the azaps — poorly trained, poorly equipped, and absolutely everywhere. They found a weak spot in the north-west, at the Blachernae wall — an older section, built in the eleventh century. They actually broke through. For a moment, the city was breached. Then the defenders counter-attacked and threw them back. Wave three: the janissaries. The elite. The best infantry in the world. And this time, the defenders were out of everything — out of men, out of arrows, out of luck. Then the moment everything turned. Giovanni Giustiniani — the man who had organized the whole defense — was hit. A bullet or a crossbow bolt, in the chest. He was carried off the walls to his ship. And when the defenders saw their commander leaving, they panicked. The Genoese started running for the harbor. Constantine, left with his Greeks, held on. But the line was breaking in a dozen places. Some men ran home to protect their families. The Venetians fell back to their ships. Some of the Genoese escaped to Galata. The rest surrendered, or jumped from the walls. And then the detail that still gets argued about. A small postern gate near the Blachernae wall — the Kerkoporta. Was it left open? Was it opened from inside? Did a handful of Ottoman soldiers climb through, get onto the wall, and raise their banner? The sources disagree.
