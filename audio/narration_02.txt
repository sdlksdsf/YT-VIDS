The walls were the whole point. As long as they stood, the city could wait for help. As long as the city could wait, the empire could breathe. The man holding the walls was Constantine XI Palaiologos. Forty-nine. A soldier, not a courtier. He had spent his life fighting for a family that was running out of empire. And he knew exactly how bad the math was. In March 1453, he ordered a census. How many men could fight? The answer, according to his chancellor George Sphrantzes: 4,773 Greeks. Two hundred foreigners. And maybe three thousand volunteers from the Italian colonies. Call it seven or eight thousand men to defend twelve miles of wall. Against an army that was about to camp outside it. Constantine did what any sensible man would do: he asked for help. He wrote to the Pope. He accepted a union of the churches that his own people hated. He sent ambassadors to every court in Europe. And Europe… sent thoughts and prayers. France and England were busy destroying each other. Spain was finishing the Reconquista. Hungary and Poland had already been crushed by the Ottomans at Varna. The Venetians promised a fleet. It left in April. It would arrive too late. Because on the other side of the map, a 19-year-old had just become sultan. Twice. Mehmed II had first taken the throne at twelve, been sent away, and come back for good in 1451. Europe celebrated. 'He's a child,' they said.
